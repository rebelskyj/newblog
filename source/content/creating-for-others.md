# Draft 1: I think that I'm going to start having drafts chronological, rather than reverse chronology.

So, as promised to one of my dear readers, I'm going to write today about an experience I had this week. As many might know, I crochet, and I love patterns. Not patterns in the crochet sense, to be clear, but patterns in the "is the world math" sense. With these two facts in mind, some might argue it was inevitable that I would make a number of spheres in different sizes and with different algorithms for construction.

The first two were made while I was out and about. The waitstaff there expressed that they would like to keep the balls.^[for reference, they are filled with memory foam, and so have a nice texture as memory foam objects tend to] Since I had no real desire to keep them myself, I happily gave them away. Later I was at the same location, again making balls, and this time the comments expressed were slightly different.

Perhaps because they had played with the ball since I had last been in the room, the comments were not "what are you doing?", or "ooh pretty", or "why do you have loose memory foam scraps in your backpack?", but were instead "you should sell these!" I found that impulse interesting, for reasons I cannot articulate intuitively. This space, then, will hopefully serve as a place for me to consider.

So, what did I feel about the statement? I think generally negative. I appreciate that people liked what I made, but feel sad that they think that all things should be sold if they are of value. Given how excited one of them was when I made a ball for them to keep, it's possible that they were being completely and totally legitimate in their claim that they would purchase one if given the chance.

Alright, that makes sense as a starting position; I generally dislike the idea that all things have monetary value, and that to express something as being dear is to express it as having a cost. That's not it, though. I'm sure that there's more that I was feeling there.

I think that part of me felt confused?

It's not hard to make the balls, in any way shape or form. It doesn't even require knowing how to make rows^[read: turning] in crochet, because it's all single crochet in the round, spiraling. I understand that not everyone knows that memory foam scraps are cheap and easily available, but I also did not know that. Now that these people know, it shouldn't be hard to recreate it themselves.

Of course, one of these people I also spoke with earlier in the week. They expressed that it feels strange when graduate students are unable to meet the demands of service work, because it's so much easier. I pointed out that, much as spectroscopy is easy to me, the service life is easy for them. They acknowledged the point, but have that trait so common amongst feminine people of assuming anything they know is easily attained. Maybe that's part of what stopped all the staff; wanting the ball is one thing, but knowing how to make it is something totally different.

It's possible that part of the impulse to pay came from a desire to avoid making bonds of obligation. We know each other solely in the context of me as customer and them as provider of good. To give a gift, then, muddies the lines in a way that may be undesirable. Because it's coming from the direction of service-worker to customer, I think that there are fewer ethical considerations, but I still understand that asking someone for something is nearly impossible. Even outside the bonds of obligation, though, there is something to be said for the idea that paying maintains the current relationship, even if it flips the specific dynamic.

Given that I live in the Midwest, there's also an incredibly easy answer; asking someone who made a craft for it for free is gauche at best. The reason I think that I struggle to say I felt negatively is that I do really appreciate that these kids^[they're all 20-24 as far as I know] saw something beautiful and instead of saying that, being art, it is worthless, they thought that it was worth compensating creators for their works. The fact that their impulse was to be willing to purchase, rather than assume in any way that I would just give things away, is a nice thought.

And, of course, I love that when I tossed the freshly-made ball at the employee, they lit up with genuine joy. I was glad that they expressly preferred the small ball, which took about thirty minutes to make while chatting with people, rather than the larger balls that took a few hours. I have no idea what happened to the ball after that; giving away a crocheted item reminds me of one of my favorite content creators. Angela Collier is^[was?] a post-doctoral researcher in astrophysics. Her channel and content, however, tend much more towards the popular science/ "I read this and have thoughts" variety, which is fun. She has a side channel devoted to reading, and one of the videos was a "I'll book vlog while making a hungry caterpillar sweater for a baby I know". It was very cute, but she also made a repeated point that once the gift was given, it was out of her control and mind what happens to it. If the mother or father hates the sweater, it can disappear and she feels no space to say that's wrong.

I quite like that approach. When I give someone something, I honestly think that I would prefer they think nothing of it and dispose of it immediately. After all, the idea that I am capable of leaving a lasting impression on someone, to the point that something I gift them brings them joy for years, is terrifying. During high school, I was overactive, as is perhaps unsurprising. Because I was also smart^[note past tense please] and willing to help other students, teachers generally let me try to find ways to amuse myself so long as they weren't actively disrupting class learning.^[there are also large elements of classism at play, and I will be honest and say I was also actively disruptive in many situations. We're talking norms, though, and classism, though important, is not immediately relevant to this story] Also as a student in high school, I sang in the choir.

Like many choirs, we went through large amounts of paper. Or, maybe this is a strange byproduct of having a teacher who demanded that we be fluent in solfege and gave every chance to meet the expectation. Regardless, I had a steady source of paper which was otherwise going to go into the recycling bin. From this, I started making origami. As mentioned at the top of this post^[look at that, unintentional foreshadowing], I like both repetition and pattern. I am positive that I made more than two things, but the only two shapes I remember making and remember how to make are little lotus flowers and small boxes. I gave many of my teachers the boxes, because they existed solely as a fidget to me. Once finished, I had no further use for them. And so, imagine my surprise when I came home during the summer before my mom died. One of my science teachers was retiring from her retirement job^[I think she was bored of not working after retiring from teaching, as best as the story is relayed to me], and my older brother and I went to visit her. We chatted, and as is expected from a teacher from high school, mostly spoke in relation to our interactions there.

As we were getting ready to leave, however, she pointed to a small paper box that she used to store paper clips. It was the box I had made for her a decade prior, and she commented about how she smiled when she saw it, thinking about how it was a gift from a student.

I do not know what impact i leave on others. I don't think that any of us can. I try to assume I leave less of an impact, if only because I worry about growing too large of an ego. At the same time, however, I think that I need to be more aware of the ways that creation can come to me more easily than others. To me, the box was under five minutes of fidgeting because I didn't want to pay attention in chemistry class.^[no, I see no irony in the fact that I hold a Ph.D. in the subject] If you had asked me whether or not I gave her the box even a week later, odds are good I would have not been able to tell you. To the teacher, however, the box meant something far more precious, if the fact that it lasted a decade and moved multiple times is any indication.

I do not control the meaning that my works have.

That's not a concept that I'm uncomfortable with, and maybe that's part of the issue I have had with the Academy when it comes to writing music.^[here Academy means formally trained musicians and my professors] I generally prefer to have minimal instructions^[I know there's another word but a quick search doesn't suggest it. Meta-text? annotation] in my piece; I see no reason that I should need to add dynamics and tempo markings. This is both because I, consciously and unconsciously, reject the idea of the Great Composer who created the music and performers which exist to enact His will, and also because I have seen a countless number of times that the annotations in a piece of music are ignored or contradicted by performers and directors.

To me, the role of the composer is to give notes and words on a page. Honestly, I think that at some level even some of that is over-kill. If I trusted people to realize chords more, then I would be far more comfortable with not giving realized parts for that. Of course, it is unfair to expect a choir to realize figured bass, and so I think for choral work it's absolutely sensible that I write out each note.

Like the service worker who cannot understand how service work is hard^[wow I'm doing great at setting up threads to bind later], I think that creation is too easy for me to be attached to what I make. I wrote half a million words^[ok technically like twenty thousand under, but] for a web serial. I do not have any part of me attached to that serial any longer.

Ope

That's freeing to admit and realize, at least. I know that for a while both writing it and not writing it felt somehow like ignoring my mother's memory. The fact that neither is true is a relief, at least. I would like to wrap it up before starting anything else on the site, if only because I don't like when books end suddenly. At the very least, I do think I owe my readers something resembling an ending, even if not the ending that initially planned.

Sorry, getting back to the point now.

Wait, what was the point? Right, creation means nothing to me because it's easy.

Thinking about how high school me crafted things constantly, drew a bunch, and did not think that either of these were key traits does explain part of it. I do find it funny that Celtic knotwork was a staple for me for so long that when I saw an old high school classmate recently they asked if I still draw them and a college classmate asked the same when I saw them. Sorry, two thousand words in gets the juices flowing somewhere fun.

Because creation has always been a thing I did as a consequence of action, rather than a goal itself, I think that it feels easy. I know that many people I have talked to cannot comprehend the idea of creating something like a book or a hat, even when I show them how simple it is. The freedom to create anything is stifling, I am well aware. I've written here more than a few times about feeling writer's block, and as though there was nothing for me to write.

I do not understand why people are shocked that I can write choral music^[or, most recently, that I'd be willing to write a piece for the choir I sing with. I know very few composers who have an easy time getting any of their work performed. By having the piece commissioned by a donor (don't ask), I guarantee that a piece I wrote is sung. What more payment could I, a composer for love not money, want?], especially when they know that I was a music major who focused in composition and early music.

When I spoke at a dissertation writing camp, I expressed how lucky I felt the students there were. They were in the throes, and likely still are, of writing their dissertation. The dissertation is somehow still one of the more sacred pieces of writing a person can create. It is made incredibly easy to put into the Library of Congress, my school maintains at least one paper and one digital edition, and I could have had a nicely professionally bound one for a reasonable fee. Despite this, most people I know feel that writing a dissertation is some kind of punishment, a last hurdle to overcome before becoming a doctor.

This is straying away from where I started, wondering about why someone offered to pay for something I created.

On the other hand, it does, more than anything, show me that the strange one in the situation was me, not them. Countless times in my life people have offered compensation or been surprised that compensation was not needed for the things that I create. To me, the things I create are generally repulsive. I've written here about how baking bread is the worst because by the time it comes out of the oven, I have smelled bread for so long I have satisfied my desires. The same is true for the hats I make. The same is mostly true for the balls I make. I'm enjoying them now solely because they're (in my mind) part of a larger piece, one that will be a reference for me to know how to make spheres of arbitrary size.^[no, I cannot explain why that's something important to me.]

To me, it's thirty minutes of fidgeting and using up yarn I don't really like. It's thirty minutes of creation, which is something I value for its own sake. To the recipient, I do not know or pretend to know what it means. It could be a stress ball, it could be a sign that someone sees them. It could be a reminder that, no matter how alone they feel, there are still people in the world who give for the sake of making someone smile. It could be a piece of trash they threw away that night.

I don't think that I have anything pithy to end with here. Instead, I'll use this space to write a little poem about crocheting spheres. Since they're repetitive, maybeeeee it will work as a villanelle.
